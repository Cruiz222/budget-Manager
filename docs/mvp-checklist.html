  • Built, and the fourth answer turned out to be the one that mattered. The three options here — hold it, reverse it, accept it — are all about money already paid. There is a fourth: refuse earlier, where _prepare already refuses, on its own stated thread that every refusal here costs nobody anything. Because the cap was not actually being enforced there against more than one request. The defect: the daily outflow cap counted PENDING rows, in the port's own words — "a cap that ignored held money would let a burst of in-flight payouts each pass on its own" — and the balance cap had no equivalent on the incoming side. So a burst of deposits each read the same untouched balance, each fit under the ceiling, and all of them settled: the same bypass, closed on the other side of the ledger. This is the ceiling whose argument is anti-mule rather than convenience, so passing it by asking twice was passing the control that matters most. The fix is the incoming mirror of the read that already existed: pending_credit_total(wallet_id, currency) sums PENDING DEPOSIT rows with no time window — a pending collection has no day, and one waiting across midnight is precisely the row that most needs counting — implemented in both adapters and summed in Python Decimal over a selected column, never SELECT SUM(), because amounts are stored as TEXT and SQLite coerces a text operand to REAL, so a hundred addends of 0.10 come back as 9.999999999999998. That hazard lands here in the same place as the outflow total and worse: this number is compared against the ceiling with >, so a hair under admits a deposit a hair over it. A second change fell out of the first: the dedupe check now runs before the ceiling in _prepare, because a client retrying a key that is still open would otherwise have its own amount counted twice and be refused for a breach that existed only because the retry was counted — answering "this account may not hold that much" to somebody who asked once and lost the response. The residual, stated rather than left to be discovered: two genuinely simultaneous requests can still both pass, because the PENDING row is written after initialize_deposit returns and not before — deliberately, since holding a write transaction across a third party's response time is an outage proportional to theirs. Closing that means reserving at intent time, which reverses a documented decision. So the settlement side is where the residue is answered: a credit that breaches the cap is applied and the crossing is named, SettlementOutcome.BALANCE_CAP_EXCEEDED, carried by the webhook's own log line and response body and counted by the reconciler. Accepting silently was the one answer not available — refusing would park the payer's money at Paystack with no automated path back, PaymentProvider having no transfer method, and a row left PENDING over money that has arrived would be a lie the reconciler repeats for ever. Not closed: a genuinely simultaneous pair still credits over the ceiling, and there is no automated way back for that money — it is a line in a log for an operator.

Safety on the way in

The doors an unauthenticated caller can knock on.

0/3

  • Built. Nine routes take a write with no bearer token, and the seven that cost this server per call now refuse at a stated rate: POST /users and POST /sessions an argon2 hash each, POST /phone-verifications a real SMS charge, POST /password-resets and POST /users/me/email-changes an SMTP round trip, and the two Google routes an outbound JWKS fetch. The refusal is a 429 in the house error shape with a Retry-After. Counters are two layers — hot in memory, flushed in deltas to a rate_limits table — so a restart does not hand anybody a fresh budget. That last claim was confirmed against a real uvicorn process, not only in tests: twelve attempts at one address give ten 401s and then a 429 carrying retry-after, a second address is untouched, the store ends up holding 14 for the address against 11 for the installation — the four refusals spent the first and not the second — and after killing the process and starting it again the next request was still a 429. That is the lifespan, the flush and the warm start all proved against a socket rather than against a call to flush(). The three */confirm routes stay unlimited on purpose: their tokens are 256 bits of CSPRNG rather than six digits, so there is nothing there to brute-force. Two things this did not close, both named in README.md: the reset route still leaks account existence by latency (decision 189 survives it intact), and the velocity control over money movement is a different question that counts ledger rows rather than requests.

The payment rail

Money in works. Money out, and the far end of both, do not.

0/5

  • PaymentProvider has three methods and initiate_transfer is deliberately not one of them — it would be a signature guessed rather than derived. So destinations are recorded and money is held, and no external transfer is ever initiated. A wallet can take a deposit and cannot send one to a bank.

    app/domain/payments/paymentProvider.pydecision 124

  • Reversing a charge means debiting a wallet that may already have spent the money, and the ledger may not go negative. The recorded shape is refuse, record, and let a person settle the shortfall — because “this account owes money” is a real state, and a negative balance would be this system pretending it has a credit facility.

    README.md “Still open”decision 130

  • The reconciler reports an abandoned checkout and leaves it PENDING on purpose, so a wallet accumulates rows that will never settle and are asked about forever. The fix is a sweep, and it needs a fifth ProviderEvent — CHARGE_EXPIRED — plus evidence the provider has stopped offering the checkout. Fail a row a minute before its payment arrives and the money has nowhere to go.

    The run reached the far end on 2026-09-18, and this is partly answered. The characters a caller may supply are a wider set than the ones we mint: = and , are in REFERENCE_ALPHABET and have never been sent, and the deliberate absence of a length rule means a caller's key is bounded only by the alphabet. A 42-character reference is not a 200-character one. The remaining check is two curl probes costing seconds — see decision 247.

Tracked, decided, not blocking

Each of these is a real gap with a written reason for leaving it. They are listed so the checklist above is honest about what it is not counting.

  • Bounding how often money may move is a second control, counting ledger rows rather than requests. The daily value cap deliberately does not count reversals, so this one has to count what that one skips — and it needs a read of its own. Rate limiting the HTTP door does not close it. transaction_repository.py:124
  • The limit-day is the server's day, so an allowance resets at the wrong midnight for anybody who is not where the server is. Africa/Lagos will usually be right, which is the worst way for a boundary bug to behave. decision 202
  • A pot's name has no normalisation — "Vacation" and "vacation" are two pots, and a name of ten thousand characters is accepted. fund.py:306
  • A session opened before an email change keeps working afterwards — decided, not overlooked. The threat that matters is an attacker holding both the password and a token, and they log in again the moment the change applies. decision 175
  • wallets and the identity tables still carry no foreign keys, so the pairing is guaranteed by writing both rows in one unit rather than by the schema. Both want the same table rebuild, and both want the backup story first. decisions 59, 84
  • Nothing re-hashes a password whose argon2 parameters have fallen behind. The migration is re-hash-on-next-successful-login, which is the only moment the plaintext is in hand — a third method on a port, not a line in an adapter.
  • Settled email_changes and sessions rows are never purged. One design job covering two tables: what the retention rule is, and whether a person ever reads the record. decision 168
  • Three receipt guards that mean the same thing are written three times, and none is derived from another. The third caller has now appeared, so the job is owed rather than pending.
  • No CLI close verb — close is reachable only over HTTP, so its confirmation prompt has never been seen by a terminal. It is a user-facing money transition with no way back and deserves its own decision first. decision 113
